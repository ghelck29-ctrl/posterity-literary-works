What it’s about

Every culture has a proverb for it. A family builds something, and three generations later it is gone.

The usual suspects are taxes, markets and lawyers. The record says otherwise. When families come apart, the documents were usually in order. What failed was everything the documents do not cover: who was prepared, who was trusted, who was told the truth, and whether anyone had practiced deciding together before it mattered.

The Family Playbook goes looking for the families that beat the proverb. A soy sauce house in Japan, sixteen generations on, that runs on a written family constitution. A father in 1920 who put his fourteen-year-old son’s allowance into a signed agreement. And the families that did not: fortunes larger than either, gone inside a single lifetime.

What it finds is uncomfortable and hopeful in equal measure. Lasting is not luck, and it is not love. It is a small set of practices that any family can learn and most never begin. This book is where you begin.

The honest half

The statistic everyone quotes, that seventy percent of family wealth transitions fail, comes from a 2003 study by Williams and Preisser. Serious researchers dispute it. The book gives you the number and the argument against it.

The honest half

None of this means a generation got poorer. Median net worth at ages 35 to 44 was higher in real terms in 2022 than in the mid-1990s. The sequence of adult life changed. Most families are still measuring with the old timetable.

Two fates

Two of the largest fortunes in American history. One is a museum. One is still a family.

The fortune that fell

Vanderbilt

1877
An estate of roughly $105 million, among the largest the country had seen.
1973
About 120 descendants gather for a reunion. By the family’s own chronicler, not one of them is a millionaire.
The fortune that held

Rockefeller

1920
On the first of May, a father and his fourteen-year-old son sign a written allowance agreement: $1.50 a week.
20% · 20%
At least a fifth saved. At least a fifth given away. In writing, at fourteen.

The 1973 account rests on a single source, Arthur T. Vanderbilt II. The 1920 memorandum is between John D. Rockefeller Jr. and John D. Rockefeller 3rd.

The difference was not the money. It was what each family practiced.

The founder

The inheritor
